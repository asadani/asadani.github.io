Voice Cloning, Measured

Cloning your own voice with Qwen3-TTS on a 4 GB GPU

Fifty minutes of narration in my own voice, on a GTX 1650, entirely offline. The model was the straightforward part. My microphone was not, and the reference recording needed to be twenty times shorter than I assumed.

September 2026 / Qwen3-TTS 0.6B / Turing sm_75 / 4 GB

Part one covers narrating with a synthetic voice: no recording, no GPU, 24 minutes of CPU for 45 minutes of audio. Start there unless you specifically need the voice to be yours.

Constraints

The hardware, deliberately modest

A GTX 1650 with 4 GB of VRAM, Turing generation, compute capability 7.5, no tensor cores. 8.5 GB of system RAM, of which two or three are usually free. This is a five-year-old gaming laptop, not a workstation.

Almost every decision below exists because of that 4 GB ceiling. The model weights take 2.2 GB, which leaves under 1.8 for everything else. The short reference, the batch size, the dtype: all of it is downstream of that number. On a 24 GB card most of this stops mattering and you can ignore me.

I mention it because "run it locally" advice usually assumes hardware that makes the advice trivial. The interesting question is what happens when you do not have that.

The Real Difficulty

Your microphone is lying to you

I needed three recording sessions to get ninety usable seconds, and none of the failures were about machine learning.

Take one was on Bluetooth earbuds. When a Bluetooth device is used as a microphone the link drops from A2DP to the hands-free profile, which caps the mic at 16 kHz sampling. That is an 8 kHz audio ceiling. Windows then upsamples to 48 kHz to fill the WAV header, so the file metadata looks perfect.

the file claims one thing, the spectrum says another
# metadata: flawless
samplerate: 48000 Hz   channels: 2   subtype: PCM_16

# actual energy by band:
   4000-8000  Hz : 26.71%
   8000-12000 Hz : 0.04%     <- nothing lives up here
  12000-16000 Hz : 0.02%

Above 8 kHz is where sibilance and consonant air live. The s, the t, the sh. A clone conditioned on band-limited audio reproduces that limitation, and you get 50 minutes of narration that sounds like a phone call.

So I switched to the laptop's built-in array microphone. Take two was worse. Same 8 kHz ceiling, because the Realtek voice DSP runs a 16 kHz capture pipeline you cannot switch off from the recorder app, and now I had also given up 23 dB of signal-to-noise and four fifths of the presence band by sitting 30 cm away.

Record on your phone

Any modern phone voice-memo app captures genuinely full-band 44.1 or 48 kHz and bypasses your laptop's audio stack entirely. Hold it a hand's width from your mouth, slightly off-axis so plosives do not hit the capsule head on. It will beat both your headset and your built-in mic, and it costs nothing.

Verification

Measure the take before you trust it

Twenty lines that would have saved me two recording sessions. Run this on every take before you spend any GPU time.

the checks that matter
d, sr = sf.read("reference.wav")
if d.ndim > 1: d = d.mean(1)

peak  = float(np.max(np.abs(d)))
rms   = float(np.sqrt(np.mean(d**2)))
win   = int(sr * 0.05)
frames = [d[i:i+win] for i in range(0, len(d)-win, win)]
floor = float(np.percentile([np.sqrt(np.mean(f**2)) for f in frames], 5))

print(f"SNR approx {20*np.log10(rms/max(floor,1e-9)):.1f} dB")
print(f"clipped {int(np.sum(np.abs(d) > 0.999))}")

# and the one that catches the Bluetooth trap:
S = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(d * np.hanning(len(d))))
f = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(d), 1/sr)
c = np.cumsum(S) / S.sum()
print(f"99.9% of energy below {f[np.searchsorted(c, 0.999)]:.0f} Hz")

Peak below 0.99 with zero clipped samples. SNR above roughly 25 dB, which is about the room rather than the microphone. And that last line: you want it reading 15,000 or higher. Mine read 7,943, twice, from two different microphones, before I understood what I was looking at.

One thing on the reading itself. Read at your normal pace, not slower. The clone copies your rhythm, so a carefully-slow take gives you a carefully-slow narrator for every article you ever render. And do not restart on a stumble. A fluffed word costs nothing. Three increasingly stiff takes cost you the voice.

The Counterintuitive Part

Then throw most of it away

I recorded 85 seconds with a matching transcript, which felt like a rich conditioning signal. Qwen3-TTS advertises cloning from three seconds, so I assumed more would be better.

It was not. It was catastrophically worse.

Reference length RTF Peak VRAM Outcome
85.1 s 9.7x 3.90 GB thrashed, then stalled
4.52 s 4.6x 2.68 GB stable, no paging

The model runs at a 12 Hz acoustic token rate. So 85 seconds of reference is roughly a thousand tokens sitting in the KV cache for the entire generation, before a single word of output exists. On a card with 1.8 GB of headroom that is the difference between computing and paging over PCIe.

The long-reference run peaked at 3.90 GB against a 4.00 GB card and spent twenty minutes on a four-second line. The short one held 2.68 GB and finished in 23 seconds. Quality was indistinguishable to me on an A/B.

Pick one clean complete sentence, cut on silence at both ends, save the exact transcript beside it. Four to ten seconds. That is the whole reference.

It needs the words, not just the audio

Qwen3-TTS clones in-context, so it refuses to run without a transcript of the reference. There is an x_vector_only_mode that skips it, documented as lower quality. I take the refusal as a good sign: it is genuinely aligning text to audio rather than pulling a speaker embedding and hoping.

Setup

The CPU wheel that pip hands you

Installing the package resolves a CPU-only torch. Silently. This is the second time this pattern has cost me an afternoon, so it is now the first thing I check after any install that touches torch.

check, then repair
pip install -U qwen-tts

python -c "import torch; print(torch.__version__, torch.version.cuda)"
# 2.13.0+cpu  None        <- the trap

pip install --force-reinstall torch==2.13.0 torchaudio \
    --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cu126

python -c "import torch; print(torch.cuda.get_arch_list())"
# ['sm_50','sm_60','sm_61','sm_70','sm_75','sm_80','sm_86','sm_90']

That second check matters on older hardware. Turing is sm_75. CUDA 13 dropped several older architectures, so on a pre-Ampere card I use the cu126 index rather than the newest available. Two seconds of verification against an hour of confusing failures.

Precision

Why fp16 crashes on old cards

The official example uses torch.bfloat16. Turing has no native bf16, so PyTorch emulates it, and fp16 is the genuinely native path. I switched to fp16 for the speed. It was the wrong call and the failure mode is unpleasant.

what fp16 gets you
File "transformers/generation/utils.py", line 2787, in _sample
torch.AcceleratorError: CUDA error: device-side assert triggered

The model was trained in bf16, whose exponent range is far wider than fp16's. In fp16 the logits overflow inside the sampling loop, sampling returns an out-of-range index, the embedding lookup asserts on device, and the whole process dies. The traceback points at the sampling loop but device-side asserts report asynchronously, so the location is not trustworthy either.

Emulated bf16 is slower and correct. Take correct.

the two settings that matter on pre-Ampere hardware
model = Qwen3TTSModel.from_pretrained(
    "Qwen/Qwen3-TTS-12Hz-0.6B-Base",
    device_map="cuda:0",
    dtype=torch.bfloat16,          # NOT float16
    attn_implementation="sdpa",    # flash_attention_2 needs Ampere+
)
Throughput

Batch two, and no more

Autoregressive decode is memory-bandwidth bound, so batching normally pays for itself: you read the weights once and amortise across the batch. On a small card it pays, then abruptly stops.

Batch RTF Peak VRAM Verdict
1 4.4x 3.38 GB fits
2 2.7x 3.77 GB 1.6x faster, still fits
4 2.8x 5.07 GB over the card, pages

Batch 4 exceeds 4 GB, spills to system memory over PCIe, and hands back everything it gained. That table is the argument for tuning against peak VRAM rather than against the clock. The clock told me batch 4 was fine. The memory counter told me why it was not.

One structural note: batch across a whole document, not within a paragraph. Most paragraphs are a single chunk, so per-paragraph batching almost never fills a pair and you get batch-1 performance while believing otherwise.

Reliability

Designing for interruption

My first full render was interrupted five times. A GPU driver reset, a charger that was not seated, sleep-on-battery, a background Windows update, and a task timeout. Five different causes, and only one of them was something I could fix in code.

A three-hour unattended job on a laptop will be interrupted. Plan for it instead of hoping.

cache every chunk as it is made
for start in range(0, len(todo), self.batch):
    idxs = todo[start:start + self.batch]
    for i, samples in zip(idxs, self._generate([texts[i] for i in idxs])):
        out[i] = samples
        path = self._path(texts[i])
        if path is not None:
            # np.save appends ".npy" to a bare name, so hand it
            # an open handle and the temp file keeps our name
            tmp = path.with_suffix(".part")
            with open(tmp, "wb") as fh:
                np.save(fh, samples)
            tmp.replace(path)

Key on the chunk text plus the reference, write to a temporary name, rename into place. The rename is what stops a crash mid-write leaving a truncated file that poisons the next run. Across five interruptions this cost me a few seconds of redone work in total.

The comment in that snippet is a bug I shipped and then hit immediately: np.save appends .npy to any path that lacks it, so writing to chunk.part produced chunk.part.npy and the rename looked for a file that did not exist. It died on the very first chunk of an eighteen-file run.

Two smaller things in the same category. Fall back to batch-of-one when a batch hits OOM, because chunk lengths vary and an occasional pair will not fit even though the average does. And check your machine will not sleep: the idle timer counts user input, not GPU load, and my hibernate default was three hours, which is exactly long enough to kill a three-hour job at the worst moment.

before any long unattended run
powercfg /change standby-timeout-ac 0
powercfg /change hibernate-timeout-ac 0
Results

What it cost

Same machine, same eighteen files, same source text. I tried a second cloning model first and kept the numbers.

Engine Params Load RTF Peak VRAM Full run
Kokoro (CPU) 82M 1 s 0.53x n/a 24 min
Chatterbox 0.5B 274 s 16.8x 4.30 GB 12.7 h
Qwen3-TTS 0.6B 13 s 2.7x 3.77 GB ~3 h

Chatterbox peaked at 4.30 GB against a 4.00 GB card, and that one row explains its whole column. It paged constantly. Its cost grew with utterance length, 11.6x at four seconds and 24.2x at eighteen, which meant chopping text into 140-character fragments to keep it under the driver watchdog. It eventually triggered a GPU reset anyway.

Qwen holds 4.4x at five seconds and 4.5x at eighteen. Flat. It never leaves the card, so length stops mattering and chunks can follow paragraph boundaries instead of fighting the hardware. The 20x difference in load time is a workflow change on its own: 13 seconds means you can render one file, listen, adjust, and render again.

One expectation to set. My 45.5-minute synthetic track came out at 50.3 minutes cloned, file by file between 6% and 20% longer. Nothing was truncated. It simply speaks at a human pace, because it learned one from a human.

Closing

Is it worth it

Honestly, only if the voice being yours is the actual point.

The synthetic path costs 24 minutes of CPU, no recording, and no traps worth writing a post about. This path cost me an afternoon of debugging, three hours of GPU time per full render, three recording sessions, and a working knowledge of Bluetooth audio profiles I did not want.

What you get back is that it is your voice, it runs on hardware you already own, nothing is uploaded anywhere, and the weights are Apache 2.0. For a course or a personal blog, where the narrator being a real person is part of what you are shipping, that is a different product rather than a cheaper version of the same one.

I kept both tracks. The synthetic one still ships. I have not decided yet, and having both sitting in separate directories is the reason I get to not decide.

  • Record on your phone, then verify the spectrum before anything else.
  • Cut the reference to one clean sentence. Longer is slower and no better.
  • On pre-Ampere cards, bf16 and sdpa. fp16 will assert on device.
  • Tune batch size against peak VRAM, not against the clock.
  • Cache every chunk to disk. Long local jobs get interrupted.
  • Never let a clone run write into the directory holding your finished audio.
