Text To Speech, Measured

Narrating your writing with Kokoro

Forty-five minutes of narration costs 24 minutes of laptop CPU, or 6 minutes on a four-gigabyte GPU. I measured both. The interesting parts are the acronym tables and a PATH bug that makes CUDA look impossible.

September 2026 / Kokoro 82M / ONNX / GTX 1650

Where This Started

The number that decided it

I had eighteen chapters of written course material and wanted an audio track for it. The obvious route was a hosted TTS API. I priced it, then tried the local option first, because I wanted to know what I was giving up.

Kokoro rendered 118.5 seconds of narration in 63.4 seconds of CPU time. That is a real-time factor of 0.53, on a four-core laptop with no GPU involved. The whole 45-minute track took 24 minutes.

That number is what makes the tool useful, and it has nothing to do with quality. At 0.53x a re-render is a coffee break. I stopped treating audio as a thing I generate once and started treating it as a build artifact. Fix a sentence, run it again, done.

The Model

What Kokoro actually is

82 million parameters, shipped as ONNX, Apache 2.0 weights. Two files totalling 354 MB that you keep next to your project.

For scale, the voice-cloning model in part two is 0.6 billion parameters and needs a GPU to be practical. Kokoro is roughly a seventh the size and runs a single forward pass rather than sampling token by token. That architectural difference is the entire performance story.

Pin the expected byte sizes in your fetch script and verify after download. A truncated ONNX file fails deep inside session creation with an error that tells you nothing useful about the actual problem.

fetch, with a size check that saves an hour
# kokoro-v1.0.onnx   325,532,387 bytes
# voices-v1.0.bin     28,214,398 bytes

urllib.request.urlretrieve(BASE + name, target)
got = target.stat().st_size
if got != size:
    target.unlink()
    sys.exit("%s downloaded %d bytes, expected %d" % (name, got, size))
Voice Selection

Voices are vectors, so blend them

This is the part I nearly missed, and it turned out to matter more than any other setting.

Kokoro's voices are not separate models. They are style vectors, which means they average. A weighted blend of two presets is a real voice that no single preset gives you.

blending two voices into one
blend = None
for name, weight in zip(names, weights):
    term = kokoro.get_voice_style(name) * (weight / total)
    blend = term if blend is None else np.add(blend, term)

Which lets you write this:

on the command line
# even mix of two British male voices
--voice bm_george+bm_fable

# weighted, if you want one to dominate
--voice bf_emma*0.6+af_heart*0.4

Spend ten minutes here. Single presets tend to carry one distracting quality, some vowel or cadence that you stop being able to unhear around minute twenty of a long track. Averaging two voices sands that off. I shipped on bm_george+bm_fable after trying maybe six combinations, and the blend was better than either component.

Text Preparation

The text your model reads

Kokoro's frontend is espeak-based. It will spell technical acronyms letter by letter, and it will do it wrong. You cannot prompt your way out of this. You fix it with a substitution table applied before the model ever sees the text.

phonetic spellings, tuned for espeak
SPOKEN_ACRONYMS = [
    (r"\bAPI\b",      "ay pee eye"),
    (r"\bJSON-RPC\b", "jayson arr pee see"),
    (r"\bJSON\b",     "jayson"),
    (r"\bOAuth\b",    "oh-auth"),
    (r"\bHMAC\b",     "aitch-mack"),
    (r"\bstdio\b",    "standard eye-oh"),
]

Keep those separate from your punctuation rules. I learned this the second time round, when I added a neural-frontend engine and discovered it says "MCP" correctly on its own. Feeding it "em see pee" actively made things worse. The phonetic table is engine-specific. The punctuation table is not.

engine-neutral, always applied
SPOKEN_TYPOGRAPHY = [
    (r"\s*—\s*", ", "),      # em dash becomes a real clause pause
    (r"\s+([,.;:])", r"\1"),
    (r"\s+", " "),
]
Write numbers as words in the source

"JSON-RPC two point zero", not "JSON-RPC 2.0". Do it in the script itself rather than in a substitution rule. You will read the script aloud to check it, and you want to be reading exactly what the model reads. Every layer of transformation between your eyes and the model's input is a place for a surprise to hide.

Structure

Silence is a parameter

Feed the model one paragraph at a time and insert the gaps yourself. Synthesised pauses are inconsistent in length. Concatenated silence is exact, and you can tune it.

two constants that outperform every model setting
GAP_PARAGRAPH = 0.55   # between paragraphs
GAP_OPENING   = 0.85   # a longer beat after "Chapter nine."

for i, block in enumerate(blocks):
    samples, rate = kokoro.create(block, voice=voice, speed=speed, lang=lang)
    pieces.append(samples)
    if i < len(blocks) - 1:
        gap = GAP_OPENING if i == 0 else GAP_PARAGRAPH
        pieces.append(np.zeros(int(rate * gap), dtype=samples.dtype))

I moved those two numbers more than anything else in the pipeline. A slightly long pause reads as thinking. A slightly short one reads as rushing. There is no model parameter that gives you that control.

One more setting that matters at this level. Kokoro's default speed of 1.0 lands near 195 words per minute, which is brisk for anything explanatory. I ship at 0.9, around 170 wpm. Listeners who want it faster have a speed control in their player. Listeners who want it slower usually do not.

Benchmark

CPU or GPU

Kokoro does not need a GPU, which is most of its appeal. It will use one, and the gap is bigger than the "you don't need it" framing suggests. Same machine, same model file, same text. Only the ONNX execution provider changes.

Provider Session load Audio Compute RTF
CPU 1.1 s 118.5 s 63.4 s 0.53x
CUDA 5.0 s 118.5 s 14.7 s 0.12x

A 4.3x speedup. On the full course that is 24 minutes down to 6, on a GTX 1650 that cost less than a hosted API subscription.

Look at the load column before you reach for the GPU, though. CUDA costs 5 seconds of session setup against 1.1 for CPU. For a single short article the CPU finishes first. The GPU only starts winning past a couple of minutes of text, which is exactly the regime where you stopped caring about the wait.

The Bug Worth Knowing

Why CUDA looks broken

I spent longer on this than on everything else in the pipeline combined, and the fix is two lines.

The pip nvidia-* wheels put their DLLs in site-packages/nvidia/*/bin. The documented way to make those visible on Windows is os.add_dll_directory(). That is not sufficient. onnxruntime loads its provider DLL through a route that searches the real PATH, so the provider silently fails to load and falls back to CPU. No exception. No warning at default log level. Your job just runs four times slower than it should and you assume that is what the hardware does.

register the CUDA libraries before importing onnxruntime
root = Path(importlib.util.find_spec("nvidia").origin).parent
for sub in sorted(root.rglob("bin")):
    if sub.is_dir():
        os.environ["PATH"] = str(sub) + os.pathsep + os.environ["PATH"]
        os.add_dll_directory(str(sub))   # helps, insufficient alone

Two more things in the same area. Match the CUDA major version to your onnxruntime release: 1.29 wants CUDA 13 and looks for cublasLt64_13.dll, while 1.22 wants CUDA 12. Install the wrong nvidia-*-cu1x wheels and you get a missing-DLL error naming a file you have never heard of. On Windows, the CUDA 13 wheels had no builds at all when I tried, so I pinned onnxruntime-gpu==1.22.0 and used the CUDA 12 set.

And make an explicit device request an assertion. If --device cuda quietly falls back, you find out an hour later. Let auto degrade silently. Make anything explicit fail loudly.

fail loudly on an explicit request
active = session.get_providers()[0]
if device == "cuda" and "CUDA" not in active:
    sys.exit("Asked for CUDA but got %s" % active)
A Dead End

The DirectML detour

Before I got CUDA working I tried DirectML, because it needs no CUDA toolkit and runs on any DirectX 12 GPU. It is the obviously attractive option on a Windows laptop.

onnxruntime-directml advertises the provider, claims the node, and then dies inside Kokoro's F0 encoder on ConvTranspose with error 80070057. Because it has already claimed the node there is no CPU fallback. Nothing renders at all. It also installs over onnxruntime, since both provide the same module, so testing the alternative means uninstalling one first.

I wrote it off as "GPU acceleration does not work here" and moved on. That conclusion was wrong, and it cost me a day. DirectML does not work with this model. CUDA does. Those are different claims and I collapsed them.

Ergonomics

Making re-renders free

The change that made this pleasant to live with. Hash everything that affects the output, and skip any file whose hash has not moved.

content-addressed skipping
key = hashlib.sha256(
    ("\x00".join(blocks) + "|%s|%s|%s" % (voice, speed, lang)
     ).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()[:16]

if not force and mp3.exists() and stamps.get(cid, {}).get("k") == key:
    print("%s  unchanged, skipping" % cid)
    continue

Fix a typo in chapter twelve and only chapter twelve re-renders. Change the voice and everything does, correctly, because the voice is in the key.

One detail I got wrong first time: write the stamp file after each chapter, not at the end of the run. I wrote it once at the end, an interrupted run lost every marker it had earned, and the next run redid work it had already finished. Two lines moved, problem gone.

The last piece is normalisation. Chapters rendered separately drift in loudness, so normalise each to the same peak. A listener reaching for the volume between chapters notices; a listener who never has to does not notice anything, which is the goal.

even loudness, then encode
audio = np.concatenate(pieces)
peak = float(np.max(np.abs(audio))) or 1.0
audio = (audio / peak) * 0.89

sf.write(mp3, audio, 24_000, format="MP3",
         bitrate_mode="VARIABLE", compression_level=0.4)

That lands around 1 MB per two and a half minutes of speech. Small enough to serve from a static site without thinking about it.

Closing

The short version

Kokoro gives you a good voice for 24 minutes of laptop CPU per 45 minutes of writing, or 6 minutes if you get CUDA loaded. No account, no per-character billing, Apache 2.0 weights, nothing leaves your machine.

The model is the easy part. Your time goes into the acronym table, the two gap constants, and one afternoon convincing onnxruntime that the GPU exists.

What you do not get is your own voice. Kokoro cannot do it at any setting. That needs a cloning model, a careful recording, and roughly twenty times the compute. Whether that trade is worth it is part two.

  • Blend two voices. Single presets wear thin over a long track.
  • Write numbers as words in the source, not in a substitution rule.
  • Tune the paragraph gaps before you tune anything else.
  • If CUDA "does not work", check PATH before you believe it.
  • Hash your inputs so a re-render costs only what changed.
